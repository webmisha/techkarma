Chapter 2. Strategy, Discovery, Delivery: Three Focus Areas in a Modern Product Organization

I was fortunate to meet a mentor who introduced me to the concept of three parallel tracks of product work: Strategy, Discovery, and Delivery. This framework was a true revelation to me.

The Strategy track involved regular quarterly or bi-annual reviews to define long-term goals and the company's direction.

The Discovery track focused on exploring new initiatives and opportunities that could fuel future growth.

The Delivery track was about executing tasks in the short term—typically within one or two weeks.

This model fundamentally changed how I understood organizational work. Later research revealed this approach is widely known as the “Three Horizons of Product Work” or the “Triple Diamond”. It has become the foundation of mature product thinking in modern companies.

Organizations focused on creating real value for users and the business have long realized: effective work requires balance between strategy, research, and execution. The Strategy – Discovery – Delivery model helps avoid chaotic busyness and instead foster intentional product development.

Strategy – Why are we doing this?

Strategy sets the direction. It answers questions such as:

  • Where are we heading?
  • What are our goals, and how will we measure progress?
  • Where do we want to be in one, two, or three years?

Key strategy tools include the North Star metric, product vision, long-term roadmaps, and initiative portfolios.

Mistakes at this level mean even perfectly executed features may bring no value.

Discovery – What should we build to achieve our goals?

Discovery is about exploring the current reality:

  • What problems are users facing?
  • Which hypotheses can we test?
  • What might create real value?

Methods include interviews, observation, surveys, prototyping, usability testing, data analysis, and behavior pattern research. It’s important to understand that discovery is not a stage, but a continuous learning process.

If this step is skipped, teams may build features that no one needs.

Delivery – How do we execute quickly, reliably, and with quality?

Delivery is about implementation. It includes practices like Scrum, Kanban, CI/CD, architecture planning, sprints, tasks, and releases.

Special focus is placed on quality, security, and scalability.

However, this track is often overemphasized. Teams chase speed, forgetting that fast delivery doesn’t always equal meaningful outcomes.

Balancing the Three Tracks

When an organization focuses only on one of these three areas, specific distortions appear:

  • Focusing solely on strategy results in pretty presentations disconnected from reality.
  • Focusing only on discovery may trap teams in endless research.
  • Focusing solely on delivery leads to a “feature factory” — releasing functions that don’t change anything for users.

Strong teams maintain balance. Strategy sets the direction. Discovery keeps you on track. Delivery moves you forward.

Role Distribution

Each track requires specific focus and competencies:

  • Strategy: CPOs, VPs of Product, Heads of Product
  • Discovery: Product Managers, UX Designers, Analysts, Researchers
  • Delivery: Engineering Managers, Tech Leads, Development Teams

Practical Example

In one team I worked with, engineers released twenty new features in a single quarter. Yet none of them impacted key product metrics. Why? No user research was done. No hypotheses were tested. The strategic goal — improving new user retention — was never translated into specific product steps. The team worked hard, but achieved little.

Conclusion

The Strategy – Discovery – Delivery model helps us see product work as a coherent system. It’s simple, but it requires maturity — both organizational and personal.

To work mindfully rather than just staying busy, we must:

  • Think strategically, not just tactically,
  • Learn from users, not just fulfill requirements,
  • Deliver what makes sense, not just meet deadlines.
“Delivery is not the problem. The real problem is figuring out what to deliver.”
— Marty Cagan
