Backbone of Product Development

Author

Mikhail Sukhov didn’t plan to become an Agile coach. In fact, he didn’t even plan to work with people — he started out as a software engineer, writing code and trying to make things work. But the more systems he built, the more he realized: code isn’t the hard part. The hard part is humans.

So he shifted into project management, then into product, and finally into the messy middle where strategy meets execution. That’s where he’s stayed for the last 20 years — helping teams stop spinning and start moving.

Over the years, Mikhail has worked with more than 45 companies across Europe, from scale-ups like Avito and Yandex to global players like Pandora in Denmark. He’s helped teams launch better OKRs, build stronger discovery habits, align roadmaps with reality, and — perhaps most importantly — talk to each other like adults. His approach isn’t about importing best practices. It’s about designing systems that actually work for the people inside them.

Mikhail lives in Copenhagen now. He still writes, coaches, and helps organizations grow — not by adding more process, but by removing the noise that gets in the way of progress.

He’s also been named one of the Top 100 program management mentors on ADPList, won “Mentor of the Year” from the Recursion Foundation, and holds a few certifications that no one asks about but probably helps somehow.

Introduction

I didn’t set out to fix how companies build products. At first, I just wanted to make sure things got done.

As a project manager, I spent my days untangling timelines, smoothing dependencies, chasing blockers. The world looked like a giant Gantt chart, and my job was to keep it from falling apart. That felt important — until I noticed something odd: projects were “delivered,” but nothing really changed. We shipped features that no one used, ran retros that no one acted on, wrote roadmaps no one believed in.

So I became a product manager. If managing execution wasn’t enough, maybe the answer was upstream — in building the right thing. That helped. I learned to talk to users, test ideas, work in bets instead of backlogs. But soon, I ran into another wall: no matter how good your product sense is, it doesn’t scale across a team — let alone an entire company — unless the system allows it.

That’s when I became obsessed with systems.

I spent the next years working with dozens of companies — startups, corporates, scale-ups — asking the same questions over and over: Why do smart people build broken things? Why do talented teams slow each other down? Why is there always so much motion, and so little progress?

This book is a collection of the answers I’ve found. It’s not a framework or a dogma. It’s a map — of the real forces shaping product organizations, and how to work with them, instead of against them.

If you're a CTO, CPO, product lead, engineering manager, or just someone trying to make the system around you make more sense — I hope you find something here that helps.

Chapter 1. Efforts without results: Hidden Cost of Modern Work

Walk into any company today — a scrappy startup or a 10,000-person enterprise — and you’ll see the same thing: People are working hard. Slack is buzzing. Calendars are packed. Docs are piling up. The system looks alive. But look closer. And you’ll realize: there’s movement, but no progress.

This is not laziness. It’s not a talent gap. It’s something deeper: a structural decoupling of effort from results. And it’s one of the most expensive problems in modern work — because it's invisible. People are burning out doing things that don’t move the needle. And leadership often can’t tell the difference.

Modern organizational psychology has a name for this: surface-level busyness.

Cal Newport calls it "shallow work" — the kind that keeps you busy, responsive, and exhausted… but doesn’t create value. “The modern knowledge worker is often caught in a productivity trap — being busy without being effective.” — Cal Newport, Deep Work. It’s not your fault. Shallow work is rewarded. It's easy to measure. It looks good on dashboards. But deep work — the kind that actually drives strategy, product, or growth — is rare, vulnerable, and often invisible.

The Progress Principle — Teresa Amabile, Harvard Business School. Her study showed that the #1 driver of motivation isn’t money or praise. It’s the feeling of daily progress on meaningful work. Without it, even high-performing teams start to wither. People feel disoriented, detached, and demoralized — no matter how busy they are.

Goal Dilution Effect — Arie Kruglanski. Give someone one clear goal? They focus. Give them five? Their attention fractures. The result: cognitive overload. You’re not lazy. You’re mentally multitasking across too many fragments. You’re doing a hundred things — and finishing none.

Organizational Drag — Bain & Company. A landmark study found that up to 30% of an employee’s time is lost to internal friction: Confusing hierarchies, Endless approvals, Unclear ownership, Pointless status meetings.

This is organizational drag — not incompetence, but structure-induced inefficiency. It’s like rowing hard in a boat full of holes.

Measuring the Wrong Things — John Doerr, Measure What Matters. Companies love KPIs, OKRs, Jira tickets, throughput. But what if you’re shipping fast… in the wrong direction? “Without a clear connection between actions and outcomes, work becomes an end in itself.” — John Doerr. When output is mistaken for impact, teams are rewarded for motion, not meaning.

The Drucker Distinction. “Efficiency is doing things right. Effectiveness is doing the right things.” — Peter Drucker. Most employees are trained for efficiency — fast delivery, clean execution, good formatting. But no one teaches them what matters, or why it matters. So they optimize the wrong thing.

Teams that ship more, report more, and meet more… but deliver less. Sound familiar? This is the paradox of modern work: The harder we work, the less we accomplish.

The result: Burnout disguised as productivity, Confusion disguised as agility, Inertia disguised as alignment

This isn’t a people problem. It’s a systems problem. Over the past two decades, “busy” has become a badge of honor. An overloaded calendar means you’re important. A quiet one means you’re dispensable.

But here's the irony: The most impactful contributors are often the least visibly busy. They’re in deep thought. Quietly building. Saying “no” to the wrong work.

What Can We Do: Diagnose friction — Track not just hours worked, but where the time goes. Audit meetings — What % generate real decisions or insights? Shift from output to outcome — Reward impact, not activity. Protect deep work time — No Slack, no meetings, no status updates. Train prioritization, not just execution — Teach people how to ask “why this?” before “how fast?”

Effort is not scarce. Meaningful progress is. The future belongs not to the teams who work the hardest — but to those who can channel their effort toward what truly matters. If we want to build resilient companies, inspired teams, and better products, we have to reclaim the link between energy and impact. Because being busy is easy. Creating value — that’s the real work.

Chapter 2. Strategy, Discovery, Delivery and People

I was fortunate to meet a mentor who introduced me to the concept of three parallel tracks of product work: Strategy, Discovery, and Delivery. This framework was a true revelation to me.

The Strategy track involved regular quarterly or bi-annual reviews to define long-term goals and the company's direction. The Discovery track focused on exploring new initiatives and opportunities that could fuel future growth. The Delivery track was about executing tasks in the short term—typically within one or two weeks.

This model fundamentally changed how I understood organizational work. Later research revealed this approach is widely known as the “Three Horizons of Product Work” or the “Triple Diamond”. It has become the foundation of mature product thinking in modern companies.

Organizations focused on creating real value for users and the business have long realized: effective work requires balance between strategy, research, and execution. The Strategy – Discovery – Delivery model helps avoid chaotic busyness and instead foster intentional product development.

Strategy sets the direction. It answers questions such as: Where are we heading? What are our goals, and how will we measure progress? Where do we want to be in one, two, or three years? Key strategy tools include the North Star metric, product vision, long-term roadmaps, and initiative portfolios. Mistakes at this level mean even perfectly executed features may bring no value.

Discovery is about exploring the current reality: What problems are users facing? Which hypotheses can we test? What might create real value? Methods include interviews, observation, surveys, prototyping, usability testing, data analysis, and behavior pattern research. It’s important to understand that discovery is not a stage, but a continuous learning process. If this step is skipped, teams may build features that no one needs.

Delivery is about implementation. It includes practices like Scrum, Kanban, CI/CD, architecture planning, sprints, tasks, and releases. Special focus is placed on quality, security, and scalability. However, this track is often overemphasized. Teams chase speed, forgetting that fast delivery doesn’t always equal meaningful outcomes.

When an organization focuses only on one of these three areas, specific distortions appear: Focusing solely on strategy results in pretty presentations disconnected from reality. Focusing only on discovery may trap teams in endless research. Focusing solely on delivery leads to a “feature factory” — releasing functions that don’t change anything for users.

Strong teams maintain balance. Strategy sets the direction. Discovery keeps you on track. Delivery moves you forward. Each track requires specific focus and competencies: Strategy: CPOs, VPs of Product, Heads of Product. Discovery: Product Managers, UX Designers, Analysts, Researchers. Delivery: Engineering Managers, Tech Leads, Development Teams.

In one team I worked with, engineers released twenty new features in a single quarter. Yet none of them impacted key product metrics. Why? No user research was done. No hypotheses were tested. The strategic goal — improving new user retention — was never translated into specific product steps. The team worked hard, but achieved little.

The Strategy – Discovery – Delivery model helps us see product work as a coherent system. It’s simple, but it requires maturity — both organizational and personal. To work mindfully rather than just staying busy, we must: Think strategically, not just tactically, learn from users, not just fulfill requirements, deliver what makes sense, not just meet deadlines.

“Delivery is not the problem. The real problem is figuring out what to deliver.” — Marty Cagan

In the next chapters, we’ll dive deeper into each of the three components.

Chapter 3. Strategy: Why are we even doing this?

One of the most neglected but powerful levers in a tech organization is something deceptively simple: a strategy. Not a vague mission statement, but a real one. A technical strategy.

If you’re a technical director, or you own a piece of the infrastructure — a platform, a stack, a system — then you need a strategy. That sounds obvious, but in practice it’s rare. Most teams work reactively, and even when they say they have a strategy, it’s often just a list of goals disguised as vision.

Real strategy is harder. It forces you to think longer-term, to project out a year, three years, maybe even five. It forces you to reckon with reality. And that starts with writing something down.

I’ve found that a good technical strategy has three parts. Each builds on the last.

Start by grounding your strategy in something real: the business. Too many technical roadmaps float in space. They might be ambitious, even impressive, but they’re disconnected from why the company exists.

The first question to ask is: what are we building for? What business outcomes does our tech need to unlock? If your company has a product strategy, great — start there. If it doesn’t, talk to your CEO or CPO and triangulate what matters: revenue growth, user retention, market expansion. Your job is to turn those into constraints and direction for your part of the system.

This part is easy to skip. Don’t. The quality of your technical strategy depends entirely on how well it aligns with where the company is trying to go. Otherwise, you’ll optimize in the wrong direction.

Once you’ve anchored your thinking in the business, zoom out. Where is the world going? What forces are shaping the landscape? And what frictions exist between where you are and where you need to be?

There are two forces worth identifying here: trends and tensions.

Trends are external forces. They’re not problems, they’re gravity. They pull everything in their direction. Cloud-native architectures. AI-powered recommendations. Voice-first interfaces. When Apple releases a cheaper music plan that works only with Siri, they’re not experimenting — they’re signaling where things are headed. If you ignore trends, you risk building something that’s already obsolete.

Tensions, on the other hand, are the frictions — the gaps, the constraints. Some are internal: broken communication between product and tech, a lack of skills in core areas like infrastructure or data. Others are external: your competitors can pay engineers twice as much, or their platform is ten years ahead. Tensions are good. They show you where your leverage is. Where to invest. Where to reinvent.

It’s this layer — vision — that separates a strategy from a backlog. Anyone can make a to-do list. It takes perspective to see what’s pulling you forward and what’s holding you back.

Now, and only now, do you get to talk about projects.

The trick is to stop thinking about initiatives as features and start thinking about them as bets. Each one is a move in a game that plays out over years.

A classic example is “demonetization.” If you’ve ever been in a growing company, you’ve seen it: you start with a monolith, because it’s fast. Then, as you scale, it becomes a bottleneck. Breaking it up — into microservices, into modules, into teams — becomes not just a project, but a strategy in itself.

But you can’t just say “let’s break up the monolith.” You need a metric for how monolithic you still are. You need an owner. You need to decompose it into concrete tasks across all engineering teams. You need to track progress. This kind of work doesn’t fit in a quarter. It’s a marathon.

And this is just one project. You’ll have several. Platform rewrites. Tooling investments. Security modernization. If they matter, they need structure: owners, timelines, decomposition.

Then you need cadence. Some teams do this through OKRs — strategic projects become quarterly goals. Others review progress every six months. It doesn’t matter how you do it, as long as you do it regularly. Strategy without execution is just theater.

So if I were advising a CTO, or a platform lead, or a staff engineer asked to write a strategy, here’s what I’d say: Make a draft. Don’t overthink it — just write. Strategy becomes clearer through iteration. Start with the business. Get the inputs right. Talk to product. Talk to leadership. Map the future. Trends and tensions. What’s pushing you forward? What’s holding you back? Sketch the projects. Focus on a few big bets. Assign owners. Decompose the work. Build it into the system. Make strategy part of your operating rhythm. OKRs. Reviews. Progress updates. Make it visible. Share it with the whole tech org. Let people see where you’re headed and why.

The best technical strategies are not rigid plans. They’re living documents — part story, part map. They don’t predict the future. They shape it.

Chapter 4. Discovery: Do right things or things right?

There’s this unspoken truth in product development: most teams don’t discover anything. They build.

And to be honest, that used to be fine. When your product is small, you don’t need a “Discovery Track.” You are the discovery track. You talk to users. You change the code. You ship. You learn. Repeat.

But then your team grows. You have ten PMs. Fifty designers. Hundreds of engineers. You add process, because the chaos becomes expensive. But the moment that process turns discovery into a checkbox — something to “do” before the sprint — you’re in trouble. You’ve stopped asking why.

This is the moment most companies start looking for something like Product Discovery Sprints. But too often they copy the form without understanding the function. I want to explain why Discovery Sprints exist, and what we learned doing them.

Scrum is great at one thing: building. It’s designed to help teams deliver predictable increments of software. What it doesn’t do is tell you what to build. Or why. It just assumes you already know.

The standard answer is: “The product manager adds user stories to the backlog.” But where do those stories come from? What research? What reasoning? What tradeoffs? The Scrum Guide doesn’t say.

So most teams fudge it. They turn epics into stories, turn customer complaints into tasks, and call it “product thinking.” Designers and analysts get shoved into the process — but since their real work happens before the sprint, they’re just floating, unanchored, in someone else’s process.

This creates a weird illusion: it looks agile from the outside, but it feels like a waterfall inside. Analysts write specs. Designers hand off Figma files. PMs approve it all. Engineers build. No one asks hard questions. And no one discovers anything.

What we needed wasn’t just a better backlog grooming process. We needed a second track — something explicitly built for learning, not building.

This wasn’t a new idea. Jeff Patton had already started writing about “Dual Track Development.” One track is for Delivery: ship working software. The other is for Discovery: figure out what’s worth building before you build it.

At first, this sounds redundant. Why not just do it all in one sprint? Because the mindset is different.

Delivery is about certainty. Discovery is about doubt. In Discovery, most of your ideas will fail. That’s the point. You’re trying to learn as fast as possible which ones are garbage — so you don’t waste a quarter building the wrong thing.

In our teams, Discovery became a parallel process. It ran two sprints ahead of Delivery. The output of Discovery wasn’t “requirements,” it was validated bets — ideas that had been tested, challenged, shaped, and were now ready for serious investment.

People always ask: “Shouldn’t engineers be part of Discovery too?” Yes and no.

Engineers should be informed. They should come to Discovery reviews. They should help assess feasibility. But asking them to sit in a UX lab all day or do exploratory interviews is a terrible use of their time — and frankly, they don’t want to.

It’s the same the other way around. Most designers and analysts don’t want to sit in code standups debating edge-case test coverage. That’s fine.

What matters is not that everyone is in every meeting. What matters is that everyone shares the same context. That there’s a clear handoff — from learning to building — and that it’s based on hard thinking, not just gut feeling.

By 2018, we’d rolled out this model across most product teams in one product company. Each team had a discovery stream, running in parallel with delivery. Designers, analysts, PMs, and a tech lead. Their goal? Use the Double Diamond model — explore widely, then narrow down.

We ran cluster reviews, where multiple units would share what they were discovering: good bets, bad bets, big risks. No one was punished for killing an idea early. In fact, we celebrated it.

Here’s what those demos gave us: Shared standards for what “good” discovery looked like. Real-time feedback and challenge from peers. A place to spot weak ideas before they became expensive ones. A visible learning loop across the org. Practice asking “Why?” instead of just “When will it ship?”

If that sounds soft, it’s not. It made our roadmap better. It made our backlog sharper. It made our teams faster — not at building, but at stopping bad builds before they started.

A lot of companies treat Discovery like a pre-flight checklist. “Do we have designs? Have we run an interview? Great, let’s build.” But real discovery isn’t a task. It’s a culture.

You know you’ve built it when someone on your team raises their hand and says: “Wait — do we even know this is a real problem?” And instead of getting shut down, they get heard. You know you’ve built it when you kill a feature two weeks before release — because new evidence showed it wasn’t worth it — and people high-five you.

You know you’ve built it when your roadmap is shaped by what you learn, not just what you promised. That’s when discovery stops being a process and becomes a habit.

Chapter 5. Delivery: Predictability and Excellence

Most people think “Delivery” means writing code. It doesn’t.

Delivery is what happens when ideas stop being ideas. When bets become real. When people stop talking about what could be valuable and start building something that is. But good Delivery isn’t just shipping tickets. It’s a mindset. And like most important things in software, it’s invisible until it breaks.

I’ve seen teams that look productive — full sprints, regular demos, well-groomed backlogs — but nothing real happens. No change in the product. No movement in the metrics. No clarity about why anything is being done.

Why? Because they’re focused on activity, not outcomes.

Scrum gives you four ceremonies, three roles, three artifacts, and a comforting sense that progress can be scheduled. But process is never the point. It’s a scaffold, not a building. Sprint Planning only matters if the team cares about what they’re building. Daily Stand-ups are useful only if people use them to unblock each other, not just report status. Sprint Reviews are valuable when they generate real feedback — not when stakeholders clap politely. Retrospectives change teams only if they create space for real honesty, not vague notes about “communication.”

So the question isn’t “Do we do Scrum?” It’s “Are we getting better?” If not, then your process is just noise.

This is something the early pioneers of iterative development understood intuitively. Galileo, long before Agile was a thing, used observation and hypothesis, experiment and refinement. He didn’t wait for certainty — he learned by doing.

Shewhart and Deming turned that principle into a system: Plan – Do – Check – Act. Toyota turned it into a manufacturing revolution. Takeuchi and Nonaka wrote about it as rugby, not relay race. Jeff Sutherland took all of this and made it into Scrum.

But none of them were focused on velocity. They were focused on learning. And on building systems where learning and action were connected.

You can feel it when a team is in flow. There’s energy. Alignment. Momentum. It’s not just because they have good tools. It’s because they’ve built something harder: trust, clarity, and discipline.

Discipline isn’t the same as rigidity. It’s about keeping promises — to each other, to stakeholders, to users. It’s about showing up on time. Asking why. Saying no. Shipping something you’re proud of, not just something that passes QA.

It also requires individual strategy. Every developer, every designer, needs to know why they’re working on a task. What does success look like? How will they know it’s “done well”? If they don’t know, they’ll drift. And the team becomes just a loose federation of freelancers with shared stand-ups.

When Delivery breaks down, it usually breaks down in one of two ways: Chaos – Lots of activity, no outcomes. Bugs everywhere. People burned out. No one knows what anyone else is doing. Paralysis – Perfect process. Groomed backlogs. Jira boards. But no one is asking why. You ship what no one asked for. You plan what you don’t deliver. It looks neat on the outside and hollow on the inside.

Both are symptoms of the same disease: lack of awareness.

You can’t outsource Delivery to Jira. You can’t automate clarity. And you definitely can’t fake alignment. Real Delivery comes from deliberate action. Thinking about priorities. Stopping to ask: What actually matters this week?

The best teams I’ve seen treat Delivery not as a phase, but as a culture.

A culture of ownership. Where people don’t say “not my job.”

A culture of feedback. Where you show your work, not just when it’s finished.

A culture of reflection. Where retrospectives aren’t therapy, but insight.

A culture of honesty. Where someone can say, “We missed it — and here’s why.”

You don’t get that culture from one offsite. You get it from repetition. One good planning session. One real retro. One tough conversation. Then another. Then a hundred more. Each one a brick in a system that works.

Strategy. Discovery. Delivery. It’s all connected. Strategy tells you where you’re going and why. Discovery helps you figure out what’s worth solving. Delivery is how you bring it to life — with focus, discipline, and integrity.

If any one of them is weak, the whole system suffers. But if each part is strong — if the team thinks clearly, acts deliberately, and reflects honestly — then what you have isn’t just a team. You have a product system. And systems like that don’t just deliver tasks. They win.

Chapter 6. People Track: The Missing Piece

In most product orgs, we talk about Strategy, Discovery, Delivery as the three essential pillars. That’s true. But it’s also incomplete. Because teams don’t build themselves. They don’t grow on their own. They’re not batteries you can just plug in and expect to power the roadmap.

Behind every strategic decision, behind every ticket closed, there’s a person. And if you ignore how that person grows, thinks, struggles, and levels up — you’re not building a system. You’re building a machine with no maintenance plan. Eventually, it breaks.

That’s why mature organizations don’t stop at the three pillars. They add a fourth one: People. People Track isn’t about HR perks or engagement surveys. It’s not about “soft stuff.” It’s about building a system — just as structured as your delivery pipeline — but designed for growing people.

If Strategy asks why, Discovery asks what, and Delivery asks how, then People ask the most fundamental question of all: who? Who is building this? Who will take it further? Who do we become along the way?

You can’t separate product growth from personal growth. And yet most companies try. Start with ladders — not titles, but behaviors. What does a Senior engineer actually do that's different from a Mid? Not just “more experience,” but more impact. Broader scope. Less need for hand-holding. More clarity under uncertainty. These are not just words — they are design constraints for your org.

If you don’t define these, your team will. Informally. Politically. Unconsciously. And when people can’t see the rules, they stop playing. The ladder isn’t a checklist. It’s a map. And a map only works when paired with direction. This is the part most orgs miss. You wouldn’t run a product without a roadmap. So why run a person without one?

Every engineer, every designer, every manager needs their own Big Picture — a sense of where they’re headed, what they’re great at, and what they want to learn next. Otherwise, they drift. Sometimes quietly. Sometimes all the way to another company.

We often say “people don’t leave companies, they leave managers.” But what they really leave is ambiguity. About their path. Their purpose. Their growth. If you want retention, forget ping pong tables. Start with clarity. The most powerful practice in people development is also the least flashy: one-on-ones.

Not status meetings. Not check-ins. Real conversations about what’s working, what’s not, and what’s next. Do them weekly or biweekly. Make them sacred. Don’t cancel them when it’s busy — that’s when they matter most. When done right, 1:1s are where career strategy gets built in real time. Where trust compounds. Where feedback doesn’t have to wait for a performance review. It’s a place to zoom out.

One of the best compliments I’ve heard about a 1:1: “I came for answers. I stayed because I started bringing questions.” Eventually, people want to grow. And when they do, you need a system. Not a gut feeling. Not politics. A system.

That system starts with career levels. Then it adds StarMaps — visualizations of skills across the team. Where are we strong? Where are we weak? Who is ready for more?

Then, every 6 months (or quarter), you run a feedback cycle — a 360 review. Each engineer reflects: what they’ve shipped, what they’ve learned, where they struggled. Teammates give input. Managers review the whole picture.

The magic happens at calibration. That’s when leaders come together and ask, honestly: Is this person ready to level up? If so, we promote — not just for past performance, but future readiness. It’s a loop. And like any good loop, it gets better with time.

If you’re a CTO or tech director, your job isn’t just to build systems of code. It’s to build systems of growth.

That means three things: Ladders — clear paths for engineers and managers, defined by impact and scope. Development cycles — StarMaps, individual plans, regular 1:1s. Feedback and calibration — structured reviews, growth conversations, and honest promotion decisions.

This isn’t a “nice-to-have.” This is your org’s strategy. Without it, people burn out, plateau, or leave. With it, they thrive — and so does your product.

Here’s what I’ve seen when the People Track works: Engineers start owning their growth. They don’t wait to be told. Managers become coaches, not just status-checkers. Promotions feel earned, not negotiated. Retention improves — not because people are trapped, but because they’re invested. Feedback becomes a norm, not an event.

You stop treating people like “resources.” You start treating them like systems — complex, evolving, high-potential systems. And that changes everything. If you don’t build a system for growth, your best people will outgrow you. They won’t say it. They’ll just leave. Or worse, stay and stagnate.

But if you build that system — the fourth track — your company becomes something rare: a place where people don’t just ship good products. They become great at what they do. And that’s the most compounding advantage you can have.

Chapter 7. Maturity Model: You Can’t Scale What You Can’t See

Everyone wants scale. Few are willing to slow down long enough to ask: “Are we even ready for it?” That’s what the Maturity Model is for. Not to sound corporate. Not to please auditors. But to see clearly. Because if you don’t know where you are, no roadmap will get you where you want to be.

It’s easy to confuse motion with progress. Especially in tech. You launch. You scale. You hire. You sprint. But under the surface, things get messy: One team does retros. One doesn’t. One validates hypotheses. Another ships based on gut. One documents. The other improvises.

Soon, you stop building a company. You start building a patchwork. And the bigger it gets, the more brittle it becomes.

What you need isn’t another process. You need a mirror. A good maturity model isn’t a gradebook. It’s a diagnostic tool — like an engineer’s multimeter. It tells you: Where you’re strong. Where you’re vulnerable. Where small tweaks could unlock big leverage.

Think of it as a heat map. It shows not just what’s broken, but what’s working, and how far each team is from the baseline.

Not to judge. To align. The model maps maturity across four key tracks: Strategy – Do we know where we’re going? Discovery – Are we solving the right problems? Delivery – Can we consistently ship value? People – Are we growing the people who do the work?

For each, you define levels from 0 (no practice) to 3 (integrated system). It’s not about perfection. It’s about clarity of state.

Example: Discovery
Level 0: We ship from backlog chaos.
Level 1: We filter ideas, but rarely validate.
Level 2: Hypotheses, user research, A/B testing.
Level 3: Strategic use of discovery to open new markets.

You see the difference: not in tools, but in mindset and repeatability.

Here’s what not to do: Use it to compare teams. Punish low scores. Rank engineers. That’s a fast track to resentment and compliance theater. Here’s what to do: Use it as a conversation starter. A map for targeted improvement. A common language between tech, product, and leadership.

The process is simple: Self-assess each area quarterly. Validate with an expert. Visualize your scores. Choose 1–2 growth areas. Link them to OKRs. Reassess in 3 months.

That’s it. Iteration over intimidation. Most CTOs want the same things: Predictable delivery. Scalable teams. Technical excellence.

But most try to get there through tools or headcount. That’s a trap. The real levers are invisible: clarity, alignment, and consistency. The maturity model makes those visible. It’s not just a map — it’s a compass. It tells you not just what to fix, but where you’ll get the most return.

The best models evolve. They’re co-built with domain experts. They’re piloted, iterated, explained. And they’re used in context, not in a vacuum.

Want teams to use it? Make it useful. Want experts to engage? Let them teach. Want real change? Make it lightweight, visual, repeatable. Don’t aim for compliance. Aim for competence.

A team stuck at Level 1 for delivery won’t benefit from more process.
They need feedback loops and ownership. A team at Level 0 for people development doesn’t need fancy tools. They need clarity of expectations, coaching, and trust. The model doesn’t just diagnose. It guides.

Why It Works:
Focus: Instead of boiling the ocean, teams choose 1–2 levers per quarter.
Clarity: Everyone knows what Level 2 or 3 looks like.
Transparency: New hires see what “good” means from day one.
Accountability: Leaders track progress, not by gut, but by data.

It’s not fast. But it’s steady. And in a growing org, that’s how you scale without breaking yourself. A strong product org isn’t one that grows the fastest.
It’s one that grows with awareness.

Maturity models may sound boring. But they’re the technical spec for culture.

They codify the invisible systems that make everything else work. And when everyone shares the same map — even imperfectly — you go from a collection of teams to a cohesive organization. That’s when scale becomes possible. Predictable. Repeatable. And that’s when engineering becomes not just a function — but a force multiplier.

Final Thought: Maturity Is a Path, Not a Checkbox

One of the biggest mistakes teams make is thinking they can install maturity like a plugin. Copy a process, import a framework, run a few workshops — and voilà, now we’re “strategic.” It doesn’t work that way.

Maturity isn’t something you borrow from someone else’s deck. It’s not a checklist. It’s not even about best practices. It’s a way of thinking — about what matters, why it matters, and how we get there together.

Throughout this book, we looked at four parts of any real product organization: Strategy — where and why we’re going, Discovery — what’s worth building, Delivery — how we make it real, People — how we connect purpose to work.

But the important part isn’t the structure. It’s how you think about each piece. Are you asking the right questions? Do your teams understand why they’re doing what they’re doing? Are you choosing progress over motion?

Because the truth is: it’s easy to look mature. A good slide deck, some OKRs, a delivery board — and it all feels right. But if your teams are burned out, your strategy is just a slogan, and no one’s asking “why” — then it’s all theater.

Real maturity is messy. It starts with admitting you don’t know something. It shows up in retrospectives, awkward conversations, decisions to slow down so you can go faster later. It’s small, quiet moments of clarity.

You can’t skip levels. You can’t shortcut trust. But you can decide to get a little more honest about where you are, and what’s actually holding you back. Sometimes that’s the hardest part: not fixing things, but seeing them.

The strongest product teams don’t start mature. They grow into it — through reflection, alignment, curiosity, and ownership. And they don’t chase scale just for the sake of it. Because maturity isn’t about size. It’s about depth. And that’s where everything starts.
